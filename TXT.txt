Oh, this is one of my favorite topics because the answer is way more interesting than "you didn't get enough sleep."

What you're describing—feeling sleepy at roughly the same time every day, even when you've slept—is the result of two biological systems constantly competing.

The Two-Process Model of Sleep

Sleep scientists call it the Two-Process Model.

1. Sleep Pressure (Process S)

This is basically your body's "battery depletion."

The longer you're awake, the more adenosine builds up in your brain.

Think of adenosine as little "sleep molecules."

Wake up
|
|  Adenosine keeps increasing
|
|        😐
|            😴
|                💤
+-----------------------------> Time awake

When you sleep, your brain clears adenosine away.

This is also why caffeine works—it doesn't remove adenosine.

It simply blocks the receptors, so your brain can't "see" how sleepy it actually is.

Eventually the caffeine wears off, and all that accumulated adenosine hits you at once.

2. Your Circadian Rhythm (Process C)

This is your internal biological clock.

It's controlled by a tiny region called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in your brain.

It responds mostly to:

sunlight
darkness
meal timing
activity
body temperature

It releases hormones like melatonin at predictable times.

Your circadian rhythm doesn't care whether you're tired.

It only cares what time it thinks it is.

Why do people get sleepy after lunch?

This is the part most people find surprising.

There is a natural dip in alertness around 1–4 PM.

Even if you don't eat lunch.

It's built into human biology.

Your alertness throughout the day looks something like this:

Alertness

High      /\        Morning peak
         /  \
        /    \
       /      \_____
                 \
                  \   Afternoon dip
                   \__
                      \____ Evening "second wind"
                           \
                            Sleep

That's why in countries with siestas, afternoon naps actually line up pretty well with biology.

So why are you sleepy at work?

It could be several things happening together.

Sleep debt

If you've been getting 6 hours instead of your usual 7.5–8 for several days,

your brain doesn't forget.

It accumulates.

You may feel okay in the morning because cortisol helps wake you up, then crash later.

Circadian dip

If you're always getting sleepy around 2–3 PM,

that's probably your natural circadian valley.

Eating

After a large meal:

insulin rises
blood flow shifts toward digestion
hormones change

This can amplify the afternoon dip.

The meal isn't the sole cause, but it can make it worse.

Sitting still

Your brain associates

sitting
warm room
computer screen
low movement

with low arousal.

Compare that to standing outside in sunlight.

Even with the same amount of sleep, you'll usually feel much more awake.

Light

Indoor offices are surprisingly dark.

Outdoor sunlight can exceed 50,000–100,000 lux.

Typical office lighting is often only 300–500 lux.

Your brain gets a much weaker "stay awake" signal indoors.

What about sleeping during the day sometimes?

Interestingly, naps can either help or hurt depending on when and how long.

10–20 minute nap

Usually amazing.

You wake before entering deep sleep.

improved alertness
better focus
less sleep inertia
30–60 minutes

This is the awkward zone.

You often wake during deep sleep.

Result:

"I somehow feel worse."

90 minutes

A full sleep cycle.

Can work well if you have time.

Why can you be sleepy but unable to sleep later?

This confuses lots of people.

Imagine your sleep drive is 70%.

Then evening comes.

Your circadian rhythm actually gives you a boost called the "wake maintenance zone."

So even though you're tired,

your brain temporarily increases alertness.

That's why people sometimes yawn all afternoon...

...then suddenly feel wide awake at 10 PM.

This is called "Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions" (JITAIs)

This is actually an active area of research in digital health.

The idea is simple:

Don't nag people randomly.

Intervene at the exact moment they're about to make a decision.

Examples:

Arrive home → remind about bedtime.
Unlock YouTube at 4:05 AM → remind about your sleep goal.
Leave work late → adjust today's expected bedtime.
Still outside after sunrise → suggest eating before heading home next time.

Those interventions are much more likely to help than generic reminders at 9 PM.
